M p.01 L18 |                                       Abstract
M p.01 L19 |  Food crises arise from interacting drivers—conflict, poverty, climate variability
M p.01 L20 |  and economic shocks—producing acute food insecurity (AFI) among vulnerable
M p.01 L21 |  populations. Early warning systems (EWS) depend heavily on the Integrated
M p.01 L22 |  Food Security Phase Classification (IPC), the gold standard for assessing AFI
M p.01 L23 |  severity and widely used across the humanitarian system. Yet IPC assessments
M p.01 L24 |  are resource-intensive, infrequent and especially difficult in conflict-affected set-
M p.01 L25 |  tings, constraining timely decisions. We develop a machine-learning model that
M p.01 L26 |  leverages passively collected data to predict IPC phases and estimate popula-
M p.01 L27 |  tions facing high AFI (IPC Phase 3+). Trained on multi-source indicators (e.g.,
M p.01 L28 |  prices, infrastructure, conflict intensity, vegetation, night lights and weather), the
M p.01 L29 |  model correctly identifies 94.1% of food crises and attains a 77.5% success rate for
M p.01 L30 |  crises predicted 12 months ahead. We generate contemporaneous estimates, now-
M p.01 L31 |  casts and forecasts, providing a scalable complement to IPC with the potential
M p.01 L32 |  to strengthen EWS and support more timely, data-driven humanitarian action.

M p.02 L01 | Main
M p.02 L02 | Food crises result from the complex interplay of conflict, poverty, climate variability,
M p.02 L03 | extreme weather events, and economic shocks. They are marked by high and rapidly
M p.02 L04 | increasing levels of acute food insecurity (AFI) among vulnerable populations [1].
M p.02 L05 | Early warning systems (EWS) capable of anticipating food crises in a timely manner
M p.02 L06 | are essential to guide humanitarian assistance and mitigate the worst impacts of major
M p.02 L07 | disruptions in food access.
M p.02 L08 |     The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification (IPC) system is a multi-partner
M p.02 L09 | initiative designed to enhance food security and nutrition analysis and serves as a key
M p.02 L10 | resource for early warning and early action mechanisms to anticipate food crises [2].
M p.02 L11 | The IPC scale classifies vulnerable populations into five phases based on the severity
M p.02 L12 | of AFI in a geographic area: 1) Minimal, 2) Stressed, 3) Crisis, 4) Emergency, and
M p.02 L13 | 5) Catastrophe [3]. A food crisis is defined as a situation in which at least 20% of
M p.02 L14 | the population is classified in Phase 3 or above and requires urgent humanitarian
M p.02 L15 | assistance.
M p.02 L16 |     Donors consider the IPC as the gold standard for assessing the severity and scope
M p.02 L17 | of AFI within countries and for guiding the development of targeted response plans
M p.02 L18 | [2]. However, IPC operations for data collection and analysis are costly, as they rely
M p.02 L19 | on both qualitative and quantitative data, including on-the-ground assessments con-
M p.02 L20 | ducted by multiple agencies. These efforts are often hindered by challenging conditions
M p.02 L21 | for collecting high-quality data, especially in conflict-affected areas. Due to high costs
M p.02 L22 | and limited budgets, IPC assessments are not always timely or frequent and may lack
M p.02 L23 | coverage in many countries identified as at risk of food crises [4].
M p.02 L24 |     Moreover, food insecurity in fragile contexts can deteriorate rapidly—sometimes
M p.02 L25 | within weeks—as illustrated by recent crises in the Gaza Strip and Sudan [5, 6]. These
M p.02 L26 | cases highlight the urgent need for timely evidence to inform decision-making, as well
M p.02 L27 | as the importance of more frequent, ideally real-time, monitoring of food insecurity.
M p.02 L28 | While direct, real-time measurement of food security remains difficult, growing avail-
M p.02 L29 | ability of passively collected data enables monitoring of key risk factors. Leveraging
M p.02 L30 | such data can enhance the predictive capacity of EWS, facilitating earlier and more
M p.02 L31 | effective responses to emerging crises.
M p.02 L32 |     Recent studies have demonstrated the potential of machine learning (ML) models
M p.02 L33 | using passively collected data to predict poverty, malnutrition, and food insecurity
M p.02 L34 | at relatively low cost [7–10]. Building on this literature, we develop an ML model
M p.02 L35 | that integrates passively collected indicators to predict IPC phases and estimate the
M p.02 L36 | proportion of the population facing food crisis.
M p.02 L37 |     Our model forecasts AFI severity up to 12 months in advance, while also supporting
M p.02 L38 | nowcasting and contemporaneous estimation. We construct a dataset comprising 5,575
M p.02 L39 | IPC observations across 1,198 subnational areas in 29 countries from 2017 to 2022,
M p.02 L40 | enriched with geospatial, socioeconomic, and environmental covariates such as food
M p.02 L41 | prices, weather conditions, conflict events, vegetation indices, nightlight intensity, and
M p.02 L42 | market access.
M p.02 L43 |     When tested against published IPC assessments, our forecasting model correctly
M p.02 L44 | identifies 94.1% of food crisis cases, while 77.5% of cases predicted to be in crisis are
M p.02 L45 | confirmed as such 12 months later. The nowcasting and contemporaneous models show
M p.02 L46 |                                             2

M p.03 L07 |      Our paper makes two key contributions. First, our model estimates both the overall
M p.03 L08 | IPC phase and the population share in each phase.1 While most existing studies focus
M p.03 L09 | on related well-being indicators—such as asset ownership, child malnutrition, or food
M p.03 L10 | consumption scores [9–12]—only a few, including [7] and [9], attempt to predict IPC-
M p.03 L11 | like outcomes. Unlike these studies, which rely on the classifications derived from
M p.03 L12 | the Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET) and focus on a binary
M p.03 L13 | transition into crisis (Phases 3-5) from Phases 1-2, we use the IPC assessment outcomes
M p.03 L14 | themselves—the consensus estimates produced by IPC’s analytical process and partner
M p.03 L15 | agencies. This choice makes our approach both methodologically distinct and directly
M p.03 L16 | relevant to early-warning and early-action systems that rely on IPC assessments.
M p.03 L17 |      An additional advantage of the IPC data for our purposes is that the assessments
M p.03 L18 | provide not only an overall phase classification for each area but also estimates of
M p.03 L19 | the population in each phase. This allows us to predict the share of the population
M p.03 L20 | experiencing food crisis, an outcome that is highly relevant for assessing the potential
M p.03 L21 | scale of humanitarian need. At the same time, IPC assessments are conducted irregu-
M p.03 L22 | larly, and about 43% of areas are observed only once or twice during the 6-year study
M p.03 L23 | period (see Supplementary Fig. 1). This uneven longitudinal structure creates addi-
M p.03 L24 | tional challenges for predictor construction, model training, and prediction. It also
M p.03 L25 | makes transition-based modeling difficult in our setting, because many areas lack suf-
M p.03 L26 | ficient consecutive assessments to construct transition outcomes consistently across
M p.03 L27 | locations.
M p.03 L28 |      Second, we provide estimates across three temporal horizons: contemporaneous,
M p.03 L29 | nowcasting, and forecasting. Contemporaneous estimates can help fill gaps in historical
M p.03 L30 | IPC assessments for areas covered by IPC at other points in time. Nowcasting provides
M p.03 L31 | updated assessments using the most current available data, while forecasting offers lead
M p.03 L32 | time for early action. By evaluating food crisis risks across all three timeframes—past,
M p.03 L33 | present, and future—our model can help humanitarian and development actors better
M p.03 L34 | prioritize and time their interventions.

M p.04 L01 | Forecasting
M p.04 L02 | In this model, the training period spans January 2017 to December 2021, with fore-
M p.04 L03 | casts generated for January to December 2022. For each IPC assessment conducted
M p.04 L04 | in 2022, we use predictors available 12 months earlier to forecast the overall IPC
M p.04 L05 | phase and the percentage of the population in each phase one year in advance (see
M p.04 L06 | the Methods section for details on IPC phase classification).
M p.04 L07 | Prediction Performance. The model’s performance is summarized in Panel A of
M p.04 L08 | Table 1 and illustrated in Fig. 1. When the outcome variable is the percentage of
M p.04 L09 | the population experiencing food crisis, the model achieves a moderate out-of-sample
M p.04 L10 | (OOS) R2 of 0.249. While this indicates limited ability to explain variation in the
M p.04 L11 | continuous outcome, 12-month-ahead prediction of noisy socioeconomic outcomes is
M p.04 L12 | inherently challenging.2 To illustrate how these predictions could nevertheless be useful
M p.04 L13 | for prioritization, we use the predicted share of the population in food crisis to identify
M p.04 L14 | higher-risk locations. Supplementary Fig. 2 highlights areas in the top 30% of the
M p.04 L15 | predicted population share in food crisis in 2022 and compares them with areas in the
M p.04 L16 | top 30% based on the observed population share. The two maps show broadly similar
M p.04 L17 | geographic patterns. This exercise illustrates how the model could potentially serve as
M p.04 L18 | a screening and prioritization tool to identify locations warranting further monitoring
M p.04 L19 | and assessment, rather than as a precise estimator of the affected population.
M p.04 L20 |     For IPC phase classification, the overall accuracy is 65.0%, indicating that the
M p.04 L21 | model correctly forecasts IPC phases in 65.0% of test cases. Focusing specifically on
M p.04 L22 | food crisis conditions, the model achieves a sensitivity (recall) of 94.1%, meaning it
M p.04 L23 | correctly identifies 94.1% of all food crisis cases. The precision is 77.5%, indicating
M p.04 L24 | that 77.5% of predicted food crisis cases actually materialized after one year.
M p.04 L25 |     Although food crises often exhibit a protracted nature [14], we aim to ensure that
M p.04 L26 | the model’s classification performance for food crisis is not simply driven by this
M p.04 L27 | temporal stability or by reliance on static predictors. If performance were primarily
M p.04 L28 | due to the persistence of food crises, we would expect the model to perform poorly
M p.04 L29 | on instances where the IPC phase changes over time. To test this, we excluded from
M p.04 L30 | the test set all observations in which the IPC phase matched that of the most recent
M p.04 L31 | training round. The model’s performance remained strong—with an accuracy of 0.593,
M p.04 L32 | sensitivity of 0.990, and precision of 0.702 (Panel A of Table 1)—indicating that it
M p.04 L33 | captures meaningful dynamic signals rather than merely reflecting temporal inertia.
M p.04 L34 | Feature Importance. To further analyze our model and better understand the role
M p.04 L35 | of individual predictors in forecasting food crises—thereby enhancing its policy rel-
M p.04 L36 | evance—we apply SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) values to assess feature
M p.04 L37 | importance, following [15]. SHAP values quantify each feature’s contribution to the
M p.04 L38 | prediction by averaging its marginal effect across all possible feature combinations.
M p.04 L39 | Panel A of Fig. 2 displays the feature importance rankings for forecasting IPC phases.

M p.06 L01 |    The nowcasting framework adopts a cascading two-layer structure. The first layer
M p.06 L02 | uses all forecasting features from secondary data observed 12 months prior to each
M p.06 L03 | IPC assessment in the test set. The second layer enhances these predictions by incor-
M p.06 L04 | porating additional features from data observed during the 12 months leading up to
M p.06 L05 | the assessment, thereby capturing residual variation not explained by the initial layer.
M p.06 L06 | Prediction Performance. The prediction performance of the nowcasting model is
M p.06 L07 | summarized in Panel B of Table 1 and illustrated in Fig. 1. The model achieved an
M p.06 L08 | accuracy of 0.654, a sensitivity of 0.942, a precision of 0.777, and an out-of-sample
M p.06 L09 | R2 of 0.253. Even after excluding test observations that fall within the same IPC
M p.06 L10 | phase as the final round of the training set, the model maintains similar performance
M p.06 L11 | (accuracy: 0.571; sensitivity: 0.964; precision: 0.733), consistent with the results from
M p.06 L12 | the forecasting model.
M p.06 L13 |    A comparison between nowcasting and forecasting models yields a key insight: the
M p.06 L14 | performance gains from incorporating contemporaneous predictors in the nowcasting
M p.06 L15 | model are modest. This is likely because many of the risk factors that contribute to
M p.06 L16 | food crises emerge well in advance of the actual transition into severe phases. As a
M p.06 L17 | result, lagged predictors remain highly predictive and effective for forecasting IPC
M p.06 L18 | phases 12 months ahead—underscoring their practical value for early warning and
M p.06 L19 | proactive response systems.
M p.06 L20 | Feature Importance. As shown in Panel B of Fig. 2, the feature importance results
M p.06 L21 | from the second-layer nowcasting model closely mirror those of the forecasting model
M p.06 L22 | (top figure of Panel A), with WFP food price spatial dispersion and temporal changes
M p.06 L23 | emerging as the most influential predictors. Nightlight intensity also plays a key role
M p.06 L24 | in predicting IPC Phase 3 or higher.
M p.06 L25 |     Furthermore, SHAP values of predictor categories from the second layer of the now-
M p.06 L26 | casting model, shown in Panel D of Fig. 2, confirm that food price shocks remain the
M p.06 L27 | dominant predictor for Phase 4 or higher classifications, while other economic condi-
M p.06 L28 | tions gain increasing importance for predicting Phase 3 and above. Taken together with
M p.06 L29 | the feature importance results from the forecasting model, these findings underscore
M p.06 L30 | the critical need for real-time monitoring of local market food prices and nightlight

M p.08 L21 | The main models have limited ability to distinguish Phases 4 and 5 from Phase 3.

M p.08 L30 |     To improve detection of severe cases, we conduct an additional “rescue” analy-
M p.08 L31 | sis targeting observations classified as Phase 3 by the baseline model but that may
M p.08 L32 | in fact belong to Phase 4 or 5. We train an XGBoost classifier on observations in
M p.08 L33 | Phases 3–5, treating Phases 4/5 as the positive class and Phase 3 as the negative
M p.08 L34 | class. To address class imbalance, we compare unweighted, square-root balanced, and
M p.08 L35 | fully balanced specifications. For forecasting and nowcasting, classification thresholds
M p.08 L36 | are selected using pre-2022 temporal cross-validation and performance is evaluated on
M p.08 L37 | the 1,170-observation 2022 temporal holdout sample. For the contemporaneous model,

M p.10 L25 | Discussion
M p.10 L26 | In this paper, we predict food crises—as defined by IPC measures of AFI—using
M p.10 L27 | passively collected big data. The IPC is widely regarded as the gold standard for
M p.10 L28 | assessing AFI and is extensively used by humanitarian and development organizations.
M p.10 L29 | However, collecting IPC data is often costly and logistically challenging, particularly in
M p.10 L30 | fragile and conflict-affected settings. Recent advances in remote sensing have enabled
M p.10 L31 | access to large-scale, passively collected datasets that correlate with AFI risk factors.
M p.10 L32 | These developments make it possible to monitor relevant risk factors in near real-time
M p.10 L33 | and predict food crises more efficiently, at lower cost, and in regions that are otherwise
M p.10 L34 | hard to reach.
M p.10 L35 |     Our forecasting model correctly identified 94.1% of food crisis areas in the test set
M p.10 L36 | with a 12-month lead time. Among the areas flagged as at risk, 77.5% were confirmed as
M p.10 L37 | true crisis areas. These results demonstrate the feasibility of using real-time monitoring
M p.10 L38 | of risk factors to forecast food crises and highlight the potential of our predictive model
M p.10 L39 | to support EWS and facilitate timely humanitarian responses.
M p.10 L40 |     An important policy implication of this study is that earlier identification
M p.10 L41 | of emerging food insecurity can create opportunities for anticipatory action that
M p.10 L42 | may help prevent or attenuate deterioration into IPC Phase 3 or above. The 12-
M p.10 L43 | month forecasting horizon provides time for governments, humanitarian organizations,
M p.10 L44 |                                             10
===== M PAGE 11 =====
M p.11 L01 | and development partners to mobilize financing, pre-position food and therapeutic
M p.11 L02 | nutrition supplies, and expand shock-responsive social protection before household
M p.11 L03 | food-consumption gaps become severe. Because the model also predicts the propor-
M p.11 L04 | tion of the population in food crisis, its results may provide an additional indication
M p.11 L05 | of where needs are likely to be greatest and of their potential scale. Given the model’s
M p.11 L06 | more limited performance in predicting population shares, however, these estimates
M p.11 L07 | are better suited for screening and prioritization than for precisely estimating the
M p.11 L08 | number of people in need of assistance.
M p.11 L09 |     Several additional steps would be needed before the model could be used oper-
M p.11 L10 | ationally. Although we employ forward-looking time-series validation during model
M p.11 L11 | development, the final out-of-sample evaluation covers only one calendar year, 2022.
M p.11 L12 | Evaluating the model over additional years and across a wider range of crisis conditions
M p.11 L13 | would provide stronger evidence of temporal generalizability. Further improvements in
M p.11 L14 | predictive performance, model updating, data timeliness, and integration with existing
M p.11 L15 | early-warning workflows would also be necessary before operational deployment.
M p.11 L16 |     Feature-importance measures should also be interpreted cautiously. They identify
M p.11 L17 | variables that are predictive of food crises but do not establish that changing those
M p.11 L18 | variables through a particular policy would causally prevent a crisis. The model should
M p.11 L19 | therefore be viewed as an early-warning, screening, and resource-prioritization tool
M p.11 L20 | that complements, rather than replaces, IPC assessments, local contextual analysis,
M p.11 L21 | and expert judgment. Its predictions can indicate where and when further assessment
M p.11 L22 | and anticipatory action may be warranted, while the choice of intervention should be
M p.11 L23 | guided by contextual evidence on the underlying drivers of food insecurity.
M p.11 L24 | Methods
M p.11 L25 | The Gradient Boosting Method
M p.11 L26 | We compile a dataset of 5,575 observations covering 1,198 subnational areas in 29
M p.11 L27 | countries from 2017 to 2022. The unit of analysis is the subnational area and month for
M p.11 L28 | which IPC AFI measures are available. The outcome variables include both the overall
M p.11 L29 | IPC Phase and the population shares across each IPC Phase. Among the predictor
M p.11 L30 | variables, we incorporate key drivers of contemporary food crises such as conflict,
M p.11 L31 | weather shocks, and economic shocks.
M p.11 L32 |     We employ a machine learning algorithm based on Gradient Boosting [17], a widely
M p.11 L33 | used ensemble learning technique. Gradient Boosting builds a strong predictive model
M p.11 L34 | by combining multiple weak learners, typically decision trees, through iterative train-
M p.11 L35 | ing that corrects errors from previous rounds. We choose this method for its strong
M p.11 L36 | performance in classification tasks, robustness to outliers, compatibility with diverse
M p.11 L37 | data types, and built-in regularization features that help prevent overfitting. Specif-
M p.11 L38 | ically, we use the XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) implementation developed
M p.11 L39 | by [18], which is well-regarded for its computational efficiency and high accuracy.
M p.11 L40 |     We further implement an ensemble modeling approach that integrates four separate
M p.11 L41 | predictive models, as illustrated in Fig. 3. This structure mirrors the classification logic
M p.11 L42 | outlined in the FAO IPC manual [3]. Each model focuses on predicting the percentage
M p.11 L43 | of the population in a specific IPC phase threshold: Phase 2 or higher, Phase 3 or
M p.11 L44 |                                             11
===== M PAGE 12 =====
M p.12 L01 | higher, Phase 4 or higher, and Phase 5. These models collectively produce predicted
M p.12 L02 | population shares falling into each severity category for every subnational area.
M p.12 L03 |     The overall IPC phase classification is then determined using a threshold-based
M p.12 L04 | rule: if at least 20% of the population is predicted to be in Phase 5, the area is
M p.12 L05 | classified as IPC Phase 5. If fewer than 20% are predicted in Phase 5 but at least
M p.12 L06 | 20% are predicted in Phase 4 or higher, the area is classified as Phase 4, and so
M p.12 L07 | on, down to Phase 1. This procedure reflects the operational approach used in IPC
M p.12 L08 | assessments. Finally, the model-generated IPC phase labels are compared to the actual
M p.12 L09 | IPC classifications to evaluate predictive accuracy and performance.
M p.12 L10 | Performance Evaluation
M p.12 L11 | We assess model performance using out-of-sample R2 when the outcome variables
M p.12 L12 | represent the percentage of the population in food crisis (Phase 3 or above). For five-
M p.12 L13 | class IPC phase classification, accuracy is the number of correctly predicted phases
M p.12 L14 | divided by the total number of evaluated observations. We separately evaluate binary
M p.12 L15 | food-crisis classification, defining IPC Phase 3 or above as the positive class. Sensitivity
M p.12 L16 | is the proportion of actual crisis observations correctly predicted as crises. Precision
M p.12 L17 | is the proportion of predicted crisis observations that are actual crises.
M p.12 L18 | Hyperparameter Selection
M p.12 L19 | Forecasting and Nowcasting Models. Hyperparameter tuning was performed for
M p.12 L20 | forecasting, following [8]. Nowcasting and contemporaneous models were subsequently
M p.12 L21 | evaluated with fixed hyperparameters, without separate hyperparameter searches.
M p.12 L22 | For forecasting and nowcasting, January 2017 to December 2021 data were used for
M p.12 L23 | training, and January to December 2022 data were reserved for final testing.
M p.12 L24 |     Forecasting hyperparameters were selected by grid search with four expanding-
M p.12 L25 | window temporal validation folds, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 3. The first fold
M p.12 L26 | used 2017 for training and 2018 for validation. The training window then expanded
M p.12 L27 | annually, with validation in 2019, 2020 and 2021. The 2022 holdout was not used for
M p.12 L28 | hyperparameter selection.
M p.12 L29 |     Candidate configurations were ranked by their mean validation score across the
M p.12 L30 | four folds, combining accuracy, precision, sensitivity and Phase 3+ R2 . Sensitivity
M p.12 L31 | received a weight of five; each other metric received a weight of one. The search grid,
M p.12 L32 | two-stage procedure and effective parameter values are reported in the Supplementary
M p.12 L33 | Notes.
M p.12 L34 |     In the first tuning stage, we selected hyperparameters for the Phase 3+ cumu-
M p.12 L35 | lative target. In the second stage, we held this component fixed while selecting
M p.12 L36 | hyperparameters for the Phase 2+, Phase 4+, and Phase 5 cumulative targets.
M p.12 L37 |     The optimized hyperparameters include maximum tree depth, number of estima-
M p.12 L38 | tors, learning rate, subsample ratio, column sample by tree, gamma, minimum child
M p.12 L39 | weight, and scale-positive weight.
M p.12 L40 |     Building on the hyperparameter tuning and holdout validation methods described
M p.12 L41 | above, we simulate the forecasting scenario using predictors lagged by 12 months. In
M p.12 L42 | contrast, since nowcasting incorporates both current and lagged predictors, we develop
M p.12 L43 |                                             12
===== M PAGE 13 =====
M p.13 L01 | a cascading nowcasting model to complement our forecasting approach. This two-layer
M p.13 L02 | framework operates as follows: the first layer generates forecasts using only 12-month
M p.13 L03 | lagged predictors (e.g., conflict, weather, agricultural, and economic indicators); the
M p.13 L04 | second layer refines these predictions by integrating current and recent (within 12
M p.13 L05 | months) features. These features capture updated information and residual signals that
M p.13 L06 | emerge after the initial forecast. This design allows us to exploit both the structural
M p.13 L07 | robustness of the forecasting model and the immediacy of newly available data at the
M p.13 L08 | time of prediction.
M p.13 L09 |     Both layers of the nowcasting model use the forecasting-selected hyperparameters,
M p.13 L10 | without additional tuning. The cascading design also offers practical advantages by
M p.13 L11 | aligning closely with potential operational workflows. As emphasized in the IPC hand-
M p.13 L12 | book [2], timely updates are essential. Our approach enables policymakers and analysts
M p.13 L13 | to use an earlier forecast as a baseline. When new, contemporaneous data becomes
M p.13 L14 | available, only the computationally lightweight second layer needs to be executed,
M p.13 L15 | using updated features. This allows for a refined, near-real-time nowcast without re-
M p.13 L16 | running the more resource-intensive full forecasting model—minimizing computational
M p.13 L17 | cost while maximizing responsiveness.
M p.13 L18 |     Together, the integrated forecasting and two-layer nowcasting framework fulfill
M p.13 L19 | a dual role critical to effective food security monitoring and response. The forecast-
M p.13 L20 | ing component facilitates EWS activation by projecting emerging risks, while the
M p.13 L21 | nowcasting component provides timely updates that help identify geographic areas
M p.13 L22 | requiring immediate, targeted intervention based on the latest observed conditions.
M p.13 L23 | Contemporaneous Prediction Model. Contemporaneous performance was evalu-
M p.13 L24 | ated using random five-fold, row-level cross-validation over all 5,575 observations from
M p.13 L25 | January 2017 to December 2022 [11]. Observations were assigned to five equal folds
M p.13 L26 | using a fixed random seed of 0. Each iteration trained on four folds (80%; 4,460 obser-
M p.13 L27 | vations) and predicted the remaining fold (20%; 1,115 observations). Hyperparameters
M p.13 L28 | remained fixed throughout, with no separate validation split or hyperparameter search.
M p.13 L29 | Each observation received one out-of-fold prediction, and reported metrics were calcu-
M p.13 L30 | lated from the pooled predictions rather than averaged across folds. This random-fold
M p.13 L31 | evaluation differs from the 2022 temporal holdout used for forecasting and nowcasting.

M p.13 L33 | Outcome Variables

M p.13 L37 | • Phase 1: Minimal — Food security conditions are stable, and basic food needs are
M p.13 L38 |   met.
M p.13 L39 | • Phase 2: Stressed — Households have minimally adequate food consumption but
M p.13 L40 |   cannot afford some essential non-food expenditures without engaging in stress-
M p.13 L41 |   coping strategies.
M p.13 L42 |                                           13

M p.14 L01 | • Phase 3: Crisis — Households have food-consumption gaps reflected in high or
M p.14 L02 |   above-usual acute malnutrition, or barely meet minimum food needs by depleting
M p.14 L03 |   essential livelihood assets or adopting crisis-coping strategies.
M p.14 L04 | • Phase 4: Emergency — Households face large gaps in food consumption, very high
M p.14 L05 |   acute malnutrition, or excess mortality.
M p.14 L06 | • Phase 5: Catastrophe/Famine — Households experience an extreme lack of food

M p.14 L10 | ulation falling into each phase within a given geographic area. According to IPC
M p.14 L11 | Global Partners, an area is classified as being in a food crisis when at least 20% of
M p.14 L12 | its population is in IPC Phase 3 or above. This threshold serves as a key operational
M p.14 L13 | indicator for assessing the severity of food insecurity and guiding the prioritization of
M p.14 L14 | humanitarian responses.

M p.14 L16 | tification and cross-reference the IPC database to confirm these are not attributable

M p.14 L22 | 5 occur infrequently, while the majority of observations fall into Phase 3.
M p.14 L23 | Predictor Variables
M p.14 L24 | Our selection of predictor variables is guided by potential risk factors and key drivers
M p.14 L25 | of food insecurity identified in the literature (e.g., [12]), and includes several variables
M p.14 L26 | currently used by the IPC system for monitoring food crises. All variables are publicly
M p.14 L27 | available, and their sources are listed in Supplementary Table 4.
M p.14 L28 |     To integrate secondary geospatial raster data with our analytical units, we apply
M p.14 L29 | a systematic geoprocessing workflow. We begin by defining and validating the IPC
M p.14 L30 | polygonal boundaries for our areas of interest to ensure geometric integrity. All spa-
M p.14 L31 | tial datasets are standardized to the WGS 84 geographic coordinate reference system
M p.14 L32 | (EPSG:4326), with longitude and latitude expressed in degrees. Vector polygons or
M p.14 L33 | time-series raster imagery are transformed as needed for spatial overlays. Each dated
M p.14 L34 | raster file—representing specific environmental or climatic variables—undergoes a
M p.14 L35 | quality assessment to identify no-data values and to confirm suitability for analysis.
M p.14 L36 | We use geodesic area calculations for the raster–polygon overlap assessment. Given
M p.14 L37 | the variable spatial resolutions of the raster datasets, we include a pixel in a polygon’s
M p.14 L38 | aggregation if more than 50% of its area falls within that polygon.
M p.14 L39 |     We extract the data using ArcPy, the ArcGIS Python environment. For each IPC
M p.14 L40 | polygon and raster date, we isolate pixel values within the polygon boundaries and
M p.14 L41 | compute the spatial average for each variable, excluding no-data values to avoid bias.
M p.14 L42 | We apply this process across all variables and time periods, compiling the results into
M p.14 L43 |                                             14
===== M PAGE 15 =====
M p.15 L01 | a structured spatio-temporal dataset indexed by polygon and month. We then perform
M p.15 L02 | final checks to ensure the completeness and validity of the data.
M p.15 L03 |     The resulting predictor dataset includes 172 variables spanning physical geogra-
M p.15 L04 | phy, food prices, weather conditions, other economic indicators, agricultural and land
M p.15 L05 | productivity, and conflict data.
M p.15 L06 | Physical Geography. Elevation is a critical factor in climate modeling, affecting
M p.15 L07 | temperature, precipitation patterns, and ecosystem distribution. We use elevation data
M p.15 L08 | from the ECMWF ERA5-Land reanalysis [19], recorded as elevation in the predictor
M p.15 L09 | table.
M p.15 L10 |     Ruggedness, defined as the degree of elevation variability within a given area,
M p.15 L11 | captures terrain complexity and unevenness. It plays an important role in shaping
M p.15 L12 | economic geography, particularly by affecting the feasibility and cost of transporta-
M p.15 L13 | tion, trade, and productive activities. Although its impact can vary with technological
M p.15 L14 | development and economic structure, high ruggedness is frequently associated with
M p.15 L15 | limited accessibility and economic underdevelopment. We extract ruggedness values
M p.15 L16 | at the pixel level from the dataset developed by [20].
M p.15 L17 |     Distance to rivers measures the proximity of a given area to the nearest major
M p.15 L18 | river system, a factor that significantly affects transportation, agricultural productiv-
M p.15 L19 | ity, and economic development. River access facilitates irrigation, logistics, and trade,
M p.15 L20 | and has long played a strategic role in shaping settlement patterns and economic
M p.15 L21 | outcomes. We calculate this variable using ArcGIS by sourcing river shapefiles from
M p.15 L22 | the World Bank and the global hydrography dataset by [21]. For each IPC area, we
M p.15 L23 | compute the centroid distance to the nearest river, ensuring accurate spatial repre-
M p.15 L24 | sentation for subsequent analysis.
M p.15 L25 | Food Prices. Food price data are obtained from two primary sources: the World
M p.15 L26 | Food Programme (WFP) and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). From
M p.15 L27 | WFP, we utilize two monthly country-level indicators—Percentage of Price Change
M p.15 L28 | and Spatial Price Dispersion—to capture market shocks and assess the extent of mar-
M p.15 L29 | ket fragmentation. Both indicators are based on the prices of a food basket comprising
M p.15 L30 | four staple commodities: rice, wheat, maize, and vegetable oil [22].
M p.15 L31 |     The Percentage of Price Change measures the month-on-month change in food
M p.15 L32 | prices relative to the preceding month, expressed as a percentage. Spatial Price Dis-
M p.15 L33 | persion, on the other hand, is measured as the standard deviation of local market
M p.15 L34 | prices within a country, capturing geographic variability in food access. To capture
M p.15 L35 | temporal dynamics, we construct four time-series features from each of these two indi-
M p.15 L36 | cators: the 12-month lag, 12-month moving average, 12-month lagged moving average,
M p.15 L37 | and the 12-month temporal standard deviation.
M p.15 L38 |     To further quantify interannual trends and volatility in staple food prices, we use
M p.15 L39 | the FAO-GIEWS Food Price Monitoring and Analysis (FPMA) database [23]. This
M p.15 L40 | source provides monthly wholesale and retail price data for individual commodities
M p.15 L41 | across markets within a country or territory. Each recorded price is divided by its
M p.15 L42 | commodity-specific reference price to form a dimensionless price index. We do not
M p.15 L43 |                                            15
===== M PAGE 16 =====
M p.16 L01 | apply a price deflator or any other inflation adjustment. These indexed prices are aver-
M p.16 L02 | aged at the market-month level across all commodities available in that market-month,
M p.16 L03 | giving equal weight to each commodity. The data retain the market’s geographic
M p.16 L04 | coordinates and name. Markets are matched to the nearest administrative unit using
M p.16 L05 | great-circle distance, and any market located more than 100 km away from the nearest
M p.16 L06 | admin unit centroid is excluded. For the remaining markets in the dataset, we com-
M p.16 L07 | pute the 12-month lag, 12-month moving average, 12-month lagged moving average,
M p.16 L08 | and the 12-month temporal standard deviation.
M p.16 L09 |     We also construct the variable Distance to the Nearest Market using FAO FPMA
M p.16 L10 | price records [23] to provide additional insights into market accessibility for both
M p.16 L11 | agricultural producers and consumers. Using the detailed geographic coordinates
M p.16 L12 | provided in the FPMA dataset, we calculate the distance from each polygon centroid
M p.16 L13 | to the nearest market using the Haversine formula [24].
M p.16 L14 | Weather Conditions. We use the “2m temperature” variable from ERA5-Land
M p.16 L15 | monthly averaged data [25], representing air temperature at 2 meters above the sur-
M p.16 L16 | face. ERA5-Land has an approximately 9 km native resolution; the Climate Data
M p.16 L17 | Store distributes these data on a regular 0.1◦ × 0.1◦ latitude–longitude grid. We
M p.16 L18 | compute the spatial mean for each polygon and month. The model inputs distin-
M p.16 L19 | guish monthly updated 12-month rolling means (temperature 2m mean m12) from
M p.16 L20 | 12-month-lagged values (temperature 2m mean l12). The suffix m12 denotes a 12-
M p.16 L21 | month averaging window, whereas l12 denotes a 12-month lag; these specify different
M p.16 L22 | temporal operations.
M p.16 L23 |     We also use the ERA5-Land “Surface solar radiation downwards” (SSRD)
M p.16 L24 | variable [19] to characterize solar energy availability. For each polygon, we
M p.16 L25 | compute the spatial mean of daily total SSRD and then average these
M p.16 L26 | daily totals by month. The model inputs include monthly updated 12-
M p.16 L27 | month rolling means (shortwave radiation sum m12) and 12-month-lagged values
M p.16 L28 | (shortwave radiation sum l12).
M p.16 L29 |     Precipitation data include the Climate Hazards Group InfraRed Precipitation with
M p.16 L30 | Station data (CHIRPS) [26], which provides daily and monthly estimates over land
M p.16 L31 | between 50°S and 50°N at 0.05° (approximately 5 km) spatial resolution. We spa-
M p.16 L32 | tially average monthly-aggregated CHIRPS rainfall within each polygon. The resulting
M p.16 L33 | inputs include monthly updated 12-month rolling means (rainfall chirps m12) and
M p.16 L34 | 12-month-lagged values (rainfall chirps l12). ERA5-Land precipitation is included
M p.16 L35 | separately as precipitation sum m12 and precipitation sum l12, using the same
M p.16 L36 | rolling-mean and lag notation [19].
M p.16 L37 |     Temperature and CHIRPS rainfall z-scores use a fixed 2000–2020 reference
M p.16 L38 | period within each polygon. The contemporaneous fields temperature z score and
M p.16 L39 | chirps z score are annual values, repeated across observations within the same

M p.17 L03 | Other Economic Conditions. To contextualize the socioeconomic environment rel-
M p.17 L04 | evant to food security, we incorporate five country-level indicators sourced from the
M p.17 L05 | World Bank Group (WBG). These are the Consumer Price Index (CPI), GDP per
M p.17 L06 | capita (constant 2010 US$), the rescaled Logistics Performance Index (LPI) infras-
M p.17 L07 | tructure score, the Gini Index, and the Control of Corruption indicator from the
M p.17 L08 | Worldwide Governance Indicators (WGI) [27, 28].
M p.17 L09 | • CPI: We use the consumer price index level (2010 = 100; indicator code
M p.17 L10 |   FP.CPI.TOTL), which measures consumer prices relative to the reference year. We
M p.17 L11 |   retain the index level without converting it to an annual percentage change.
M p.17 L12 | • GDP per capita: Represents the average economic output per person, offering insight
M p.17 L13 |   into national wealth and economic productivity.
M p.17 L14 | • Infrastructure Index: We use the LPI component “Quality of trade and transport-
M p.17 L15 |   related infrastructure” (indicator code LP.LPI.INFR.XQ). This survey-based score
M p.17 L16 |   averages respondents’ ratings of infrastructure quality, including ports, railroads,
M p.17 L17 |   roads, and information technology, on a scale from 1 (low) to 5 (high). Our predictor
M p.17 L18 |   is the published infrastructure score divided by 5, giving a theoretical range of 0.2–
M p.17 L19 |   1, with higher values indicating better infrastructure. LPI observations are available
M p.17 L20 |   for survey years rather than every calendar year.
M p.17 L21 | • Gini Index: Quantifies income or consumption expenditure inequality, ranging from
M p.17 L22 |   0 (perfect equality) to 100 (perfect inequality), reflecting disparities within the
M p.17 L23 |   population.
M p.17 L24 | • Control of Corruption Indicator: Assesses perceptions of public power misuse for
M p.17 L25 |   private gain, covering both minor and large-scale corruption as well as state capture
M p.17 L26 |   by elites. It is expressed as a percentile rank (0–100), with higher values indicating
M p.17 L27 |   stronger control over corruption.
M p.17 L28 |     These country-level indicators enter our country-year dataset to characterize eco-
M p.17 L29 | nomic conditions, consumer-price levels, infrastructural capacity, income distribution,
M p.17 L30 | and governance quality. We also include their 12-month lagged values as additional
M p.17 L31 | predictors in the analysis.
M p.17 L32 |     Night-time light intensity provides a proxy for economic activity, urbanization, and
M p.17 L33 | human presence. We use monthly night-time light composites from the Visible Infrared
M p.17 L34 | Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) Day/Night Band [29]. These satellite observations
M p.17 L35 | provide information on economic activity where reliable monthly economic indicators
M p.17 L36 | are scarce.
M p.17 L37 |     We calculate the 12-month rolling mean and standard deviation of monthly VIIRS
M p.17 L38 | night-time light intensity as measures of development and temporal variability. We
M p.17 L39 | also include the 12-month lag of the rolling mean as a lagged predictor.
M p.17 L40 |     Travel time to cities is a key indicator of access to economic resources and urban
M p.17 L41 | centers. Sourced from Weiss (2018) [30], it maps global travel times to cities in 2015
M p.17 L42 | at a one-square-kilometer resolution. We compute travel time by extracting and
M p.17 L43 | averaging values from geographic polygons. This metric captures access to urban

M p.18 L02 | distribution. Limited connectivity can hinder livelihoods and development, under-
M p.18 L03 | scoring the importance of infrastructure for economic growth.
M p.18 L04 | Agriculture and Land Productivity. We use the Global OCO-2 SIF (Solar-
M p.18 L05 | Induced Fluorescence) based Gross Primary Production (GOSIF-GPP) and the
M p.18 L06 | Enhanced Vegetation Index (EVI) to monitor land productivity dynamics. GOSIF-
M p.18 L07 | GPP estimates the total carbon dioxide converted to organic carbon via photosynthesis
M p.18 L08 | and serves as a high-resolution proxy for terrestrial productivity. It integrates data
M p.18 L09 | from: (1) OCO-2 SIF, a direct indicator of photosynthetic activity, (2) MODIS
M p.18 L10 | EVI, which reflects vegetation health, and (3) MERRA-2 meteorological data, which
M p.18 L11 | accounts for climatic influences.
M p.18 L12 |     The GOSIF-GPP dataset offers monthly estimates at a 0.05° spatial resolution
M p.18 L13 | and is highly correlated with ground-based GPP measurements, providing insights
M p.18 L14 | into ecosystem productivity and health [31]. For each geographic unit, we calculate
M p.18 L15 | the spatial mean and construct: (1) a 12-month rolling average to capture long-term
M p.18 L16 | trends, (2) the 12-month standard deviation to assess seasonal variability and crop
M p.18 L17 | stress, and (3) a 12-month lag of the rolling average to serve as a predictive indicator
M p.18 L18 | of delayed productivity responses.
M p.18 L19 |     EVI is a satellite-derived vegetation index designed to quantify greenness and plant
M p.18 L20 | health with improved sensitivity over traditional indices, particularly in densely veg-
M p.18 L21 | etated regions [32]. Derived from MODIS sensors at a 250m resolution, it enables
M p.18 L22 | detailed analysis of vegetation dynamics and responses to environmental changes.
M p.18 L23 |     For our analysis, we extract 16-day EVI raster files based on predefined geographic
M p.18 L24 | shapes and integrate them into our dataset. From these, we compute: (1) a 12-month
M p.18 L25 | moving average to identify long-term vegetation trends, (2) the 12-month standard
M p.18 L26 | deviation to capture seasonal fluctuations, and (3) a 12-month lag of the rolling average
M p.18 L27 | to reflect lagged vegetation conditions.
M p.18 L28 |     Soil moisture is a key indicator of drought conditions and plays a critical role
M p.18 L29 | in agricultural planning and ecosystem health. We incorporate monthly average soil
M p.18 L30 | moisture data from ERA5-Land [19], represented by soil moisture mean and its
M p.18 L31 | derived predictors. We compute a 12-month rolling mean and standard deviation to
M p.18 L32 | capture long-term trends and periods of high variability that may affect plant growth
M p.18 L33 | and water availability. In addition, we include a 12-month lag of the rolling average
M p.18 L34 | to serve as a lagged predictive indicator of soil conditions.
M p.18 L35 |     We also integrate soil property data from the International Soil Reference and
M p.18 L36 | Information Centre (ISRIC) SoilGrids, a global digital soil mapping platform that
M p.18 L37 | employs advanced machine learning techniques [33]. SoilGrids provides global esti-
M p.18 L38 | mates of soil properties at six standard depth intervals with 250-meter spatial
M p.18 L39 | resolution. Available attributes include pH, soil organic carbon content, bulk density,
M p.18 L40 | coarse fragment content, sand, silt, and clay composition, cation exchange capacity
M p.18 L41 | (CEC), total nitrogen, soil organic carbon density, and carbon stock. For our analy-
M p.18 L42 | sis, we extract the spatial mean of each property at the near-surface depth (0–5 cm)
M p.18 L43 | using raster data.

M p.19 L03 | oped and maintained by the European Commission’s Joint Research Centre (JRC).

M p.19 L06 | dataset as two separate variables: the percentage of land area classified as cropland
M p.19 L07 | and the percentage classified as rangeland.
M p.19 L08 |     Furthermore, we incorporate Agro-Ecological Zones (AEZ) from [35], which clas-

M p.19 L16 | of Tanzania; Tropical Monsoon Bimodal (TMB), covering Kenya, coastal Tanzania,
M p.19 L17 | and Haiti; Tropical Monsoon Rice/Irrigated (TMR/TMI), found in Bangladesh;
M p.19 L18 | Tropical Highland Mixed (THR), present in Ethiopia and Guatemala; Hot Arid Irri-
M p.19 L19 | gated (HAI), spanning parts of Sudan and Pakistan; Hot Semi-Arid Winter-Cereal
M p.19 L20 | (HSW), covering Afghanistan and western Pakistan; Tropical Rainforest Bimodal
M p.19 L21 | (TRB), occurring in the Democratic Republic of Congo and eastern Andean Ecuador;
M p.19 L22 | Mediterranean Winter Cereal (MED), located around Lebanon and Palestine; and
M p.19 L23 | Temperate Southern Hemisphere (TSH), found in South Africa and Lesotho.
M p.19 L24 | Conflict. Conflict is a major driver of instability and insecurity. To measure its
M p.19 L25 | impact, we use two key metrics:
M p.19 L26 | • Conflict Incidence: Monthly counts of violent events, recorded separately for each
M p.19 L27 |   event type.
M p.19 L28 | • Conflict Severity: Monthly fatality counts, recorded separately for the same event
M p.19 L29 |   types.
M p.19 L30 | These metrics are derived from the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data (ACLED)
M p.19 L31 | dataset [36], which provides precise geographic coordinates for conflict events. Using
M p.19 L32 | these coordinates, we calculate distances from conflict points to the polygon centroids
M p.19 L33 | of sampled areas.
M p.19 L34 |     To generate the inverse distance-weighted frequencies, we multiply the total num-
M p.19 L35 | ber of conflicts or fatalities at each location by the inverse of its distance to the
M p.19 L36 | centroid. This produces the monthly conflict and fatality frequencies, weighted by
M p.19 L37 | proximity. We apply this calculation using the 5 and 10 nearest conflict locations,
M p.19 L38 | representing different spatial scales of influence. Smaller numbers indicate a stronger
M p.19 L39 | weighting on nearby events.
M p.19 L40 |     We analyze various types of conflict events, including:
M p.19 L41 | • Battles: Counts and fatalities from direct combat, providing insights into the
M p.19 L42 |   intensity of armed engagements.

M p.20 L01 | • Explosions: Counts and deaths caused by explosive devices, reflecting the human
M p.20 L02 |   cost of such tactics.
M p.20 L03 | • Violence Against Civilians: Counts and fatalities from targeted attacks on non-
M p.20 L04 |   combatant populations, highlighting the toll on civilians.
M p.20 L05 |    For each event type, the inputs retain monthly counts and the corre-
M p.20 L06 | sponding distance-weighted variants, identified by the suffixes w5 and w10.

M p.41 L02 | We report the hyperparameter search space, temporal validation procedure, model-

M p.41 L04 | Hyperparameter tuning
M p.41 L05 | Hyperparameter tuning was performed for forecasting. Nowcasting and contemporane-
M p.41 L06 | ous models were subsequently evaluated with fixed hyperparameters, without separate
M p.41 L07 | hyperparameter searches. The forecasting search exhaustively enumerated the Carte-
M p.41 L08 | sian product of the eight hyperparameter grids shown below; it was not based on
M p.41 L09 | random sampling. Tuning was conducted in two stages. We first selected the parame-
M p.41 L10 | ters for the Phase 3+ cumulative target. In the second stage, the Phase 3+ component
M p.41 L11 | was held fixed while parameter settings were selected for the Phase 2+, Phase 4+,
M p.41 L12 | and Phase 5 cumulative targets. All 11,664 candidate configurations were evaluated
M p.41 L13 | in each stage, corresponding to 23,328 candidate-configuration evaluations across the
M p.41 L14 | two searches.
M p.41 L15 | Temporal validation and selection criterion
M p.41 L16 | Each candidate configuration was evaluated using four expanding-window temporal
M p.41 L17 | validation folds: 2017 was used to train the first fold and 2018 for validation; 2017–2018
M p.41 L18 | were used for training and 2019 for validation; 2017–2019 were used for training
M p.41 L19 | and 2020 for validation; and 2017–2020 were used for training and 2021 for valida-
M p.41 L20 | tion. The 2022 data were reserved exclusively for final holdout evaluation. Candidate
M p.41 L21 | configurations were ranked by their mean validation score across the four folds:
M p.41 L22 |                                                                     2
M p.41 L23 |         Selection score = Accuracy + Precision + 5 × Sensitivity + RPhase 3+

M p.41 L26 | parameter values used to produce the reported predictions are listed below. The two
M p.41 L27 | layers of the nowcasting model used the same parameter settings. The contemporane-
M p.41 L28 | ous model used a common parameter set for all four cumulative targets. For the final
M p.41 L29 | forecasting and nowcasting models, the Phase 2+ cumulative target used max depth =
